DADL: A Detector-Aware Data Layer for Single-Photon Experiments

Making detector imperfections known, bounded and auditable

The DADL contributors

Technical report · Version 0.0.2 · November 2026 · github.com/usecommon/dadl

Abstract

Results in quantum optics, from Bell tests and Hong–Ou–Mandel interference to state tomography and quantum key distribution, are computed from coincidence counts recorded by imperfect single-photon detectors. Efficiency loss, dark counts, dead time, afterpulsing, crosstalk and timing drift all shift the reported numbers, yet the corrections applied for them are rarely recorded, rarely carried into the stated uncertainty, and occasionally large enough to manufacture a result. We describe DADL, an open-source data layer designed to keep a versioned, uncertainty-carrying model of every detector channel, compute analyses through a single forward model of the detection process, always report raw and corrected values together, and refuse corrections that are invalid for the analysis at hand. The first phase, available now, provides a detector simulator, raw Bell and visibility analyses on simulated or recorded time tags, and the guardrails; calibration and corrected values follow. We explain the problem it addresses, how it is used in an experiment, its guardrails, and its current status.

Keywords: single-photon detection; detector calibration; coincidence counting; Bell inequalities; uncertainty propagation; reproducibility.

1Introduction

A single-photon experiment does not observe photons directly. It observes time tags: the instants at which detectors produced an electrical pulse. Correlations between those time tags carry the physics, and every imperfection of the detectors enters the data before any analysis begins (Figure 1).

The methods for characterizing these effects are mature. Correlated-photon (Klyshko) calibration and conventional substitution calibration agree at NIST to within 0.14(14)%, with standard uncertainties near 0.2% [1], and the relevant vocabulary has been standardized [2]. What is missing is not physics but practice: a consistent way to apply these methods to the data of a running experiment, record what was applied, and carry the resulting uncertainty into the published number. DADL is an attempt to provide that practice as reusable, open-source software.

2Sources of error in photon counting

Error enters a coincidence measurement at every stage between the source and the analysis (Figure 2). The detector stage contributes the largest number of distinct effects, and the least visible: they depend on temperature, bias, count rate and device age, and they differ markedly between detector families.

Typical magnitudes illustrate the range. A commercial silicon SPAD module is graded between 25 and 1500 dark counts per second, with a dead time of 22–42 ns and afterpulsing of 0.5–1% [3]. A recent waveguide-integrated visible SPAD reported 25–30% afterpulsing [4]. Silicon photomultipliers show optical crosstalk of about 25% as bare devices at high bias, rising toward 40–49% with reflective packaging [5]. An analysis that ignores effects of this size is not reporting the state of the photons; it is reporting the state of the detectors.

3Failure modes in current practice

3.1 Silent drift

Dark counts rise with temperature; a reseated fiber connector changes coupling; a cable delay shifts by a hundred picoseconds as the room warms. Each degrades the result, and without a record of the detector's state alongside the data the cause cannot be identified after the fact.

3.2 Overcorrection

Corrections can create results as well as repair them. In one published example, subtracting accidental coincidences raised a measured two-photon visibility from about 45% to 82%, past the 1/√2 ≈ 71% that, for a Werner state with that visibility in two conjugate bases, implies a Bell violation [6]. In another, a classical source analysed with coincidence windows chosen from the data produced an apparent violation of over 2700σ, and no violation with predefined windows [7]. Maximum-likelihood tomography, the standard estimator, has been shown to underestimate fidelity and overestimate entanglement [8]. These are not errors of calculation; they are errors of procedure, and they recur because nothing in the usual toolchain prevents them.

3.3 Unreproducible numbers

Corrections are typically applied in notebooks or spreadsheets. Which calibration was used, from when, in what order, and with what uncertainty is seldom recorded with the result, so a published number can be neither traced to its inputs nor recomputed by a colleague or referee.

Existing software addresses parts of this chain but not the chain itself. Time-tagger software computes coincidences without a model of the detectors; detector controllers tune bias without reference to the experiment's figure of merit; tomography libraries estimate states without a record of the detectors behind the counts. No tool connects detector state, correction, uncertainty, result and explanation across vendors. That connection is the purpose of DADL.

4Design

DADL is a layer between the instruments and the analysis (Figure 3). Data arrive through vendor adapters; a registry holds a fitted, versioned model of every detector channel; calibration routines update the registry from the experiment's own data; and all corrections, analyses and explanations are computed through the same registry entries.

Three design commitments follow from Section 3. First, corrections are derived from one generative model of the detection process, fitted to the data, rather than from a sequence of independent subtractions that may count the same event twice. Second, raw values are never discarded: every result carries its raw value, its corrected value where one is permitted, and its labels. Third, explanations of a change in a result are computed numerically before any text is produced.

For a polarization-entangled source, the CHSH parameter is formed from four correlators,

S = E(a, b) − E(a, b′) + E(a′, b) + E(a′, b′),   E = (N++ + N−− − N+− − N−+) / N,(1)

and uniform accidental coincidences A dilute every correlator equally, so that for a Werner-like state of visibility V with C true coincidences

Sraw = 2√2 V · C / (C + A).(2)

Equation (2) is one of the closed-form results against which the DADL simulator and analyses are tested; with the parameters of the bundled example it predicts S = 2.686, and the simulation returns 2.689 ± 0.011 (Listing 2).

5Use in an experiment

DADL is designed to fit the measurement loop a laboratory already runs (Figure 4). Most calibration data are by-products of ordinary acquisitions: the cross-correlation peak yields channel delays and timing response, off-peak bins yield the accidental rate, and the heralding ratio yields system efficiency.

In practice a measurement proceeds as follows. The status of each step in the current release is given in italics.

  1. Describe the setup. A single configuration file names the source, each detector channel and its family, the time tagger and the analysis (Listing 1). Available: dadl init copies the example and dadl quickstart setup.yaml runs the edited copy.
  2. Declare the analysis before acquisition. The coincidence window, sample size and any data-exclusion rules are fixed in advance. For Bell-type analyses a window derived from the data is rejected, since this is the mechanism of the coincidence-time loophole [7]. Window rule available; the declared window is recorded but cannot be verified until signed analysis plans (planned).
  3. Acquire. Time tags are read from a tagger, a recorded file or the built-in simulator. Configuration changes, buffer overflows and dropped data are logged as events. Simulator and generic tag files (dadl analyze) available; vendor adapters planned.
  4. Calibrate. Dark counts and afterpulsing are fitted jointly from dark inter-arrival data; delays and timing response from the cross-correlation peak; system efficiency from the heralding ratio. Each fit is stored with its posterior uncertainty, and a new record supersedes but never overwrites the previous one. Planned; record schema available.
  5. Correct and fit. CHSH, HOM visibility and tomography are computed through the forward model, with calibration uncertainty propagated by joint Monte Carlo sampling. Every result returns its raw value first, its corrected value where permitted, and its labels (Listing 2). Raw CHSH and visibility available; corrected values planned.
  6. Explain. When a result changes between runs, the analysis is recomputed with each identifiable cause held at its earlier value (Section 7). Planned.
  7. Archive. Time tags from calibration windows and declared runs are stored in Photon-HDF5 [9] with a calibration group (under /user until a /calibration group is accepted upstream) recording which records were applied, in what order, and with which software versions. Schema available; export planned.

schema_version: 1
name: chsh-spad

6Guardrails

A tool that improves reported numbers will be used to improve reported numbers. DADL therefore treats the validity of a correction as a property of the analysis, enforced in code. Each analysis type declares which corrections may appear, always as a labelled secondary value; a forbidden correction raises an error rather than a warning. Figure 5 shows the case from Section 3.2 as it is handled by the current release.

The rules enforced in the current release are:

  • raw and corrected values are always returned together, and a result is never silently converted to a bare number (a guard against accidents, not forgery);
  • the analysis type is fixed by the function called, so data cannot be filed under a looser rule;
  • Bell-type headline values use raw counts and fixed coincidence windows declared before acquisition (in the first phase the declaration is recorded, not verified);
  • every coincidence-normalized S is labelled as assuming fair sampling;
  • analyses that DADL does not know, including third-party plugins, receive the strictest policy.

Because DADL is distributed as a starter kit, its policy can be edited. Loosening a rule requires a written reason. Every result records the fingerprint of the policy it was computed under, which covers both the rules and the thresholds, together with any overrides and their reasons, and a result computed under a non-default policy is labelled as such. The first phase trusts the counts and time tags it is given; dataset identifiers computed from raw tags are planned.

7Attribution of drift

When a result changes between runs, the usual explanation is a conjecture. DADL is designed to replace it with a computation: the analysis is repeated with each identifiable group of parameters held at its earlier value, the change is divided among those groups by Shapley attribution, and any part not reproduced by the model is reported as misfit. 8Benefits

Table 1 summarizes the practical difference DADL is intended to make to routine questions in a single-photon laboratory.

Table 1. Routine questions with and without DADL.
QuestionCommon practiceWith DADL
What is the detector efficiency now?Datasheet value or an old calibrationVersioned record from current data, with uncertainty and provenance
How large are the error bars?Poisson errors on countsCounting and calibration uncertainty propagated jointly (counting only in the first phase)
Did a correction inflate the result?Depends on the analysis scriptRaw value always reported; invalid corrections rejected; threshold crossings labelled
Why did the result change?Conjecture and re-measurementAttribution with intervals and an explicit unexplained part
Can the number be reproduced?Rarely; corrections undocumentedEach result cites its calibration records and software versions
Does it work across hardware?Each vendor tool covers its own devicesOne model across taggers and detector families; extensible adapters

The benefits differ by user. For quantum network testbeds, where availability is the figure of merit, a continuous health monitor tracks each channel, flags latching and blinding, and exports metrics to an orchestrator, with each alert linked to its evidence. For research laboratories running Bell, HOM and tomography experiments, the gains are honest uncertainties, protection against loophole-prone procedures, and less time spent rebuilding calibration scripts. For quantum key distribution developers, DADL offers detector characterization along the lines of ETSI GS QKD 011 [10] and signed monitoring records for each key session; it never alters data in the security path, and its records document what was observed rather than prove security. For teaching laboratories, the simulator injects known imperfections, so that the effect of a dark count, an afterpulse or a dead-time loss on a Bell parameter can be observed and checked against closed-form results such as Eq. (2).

9Limitations

Software cannot recover lost efficiency or timing resolution; DADL makes the imperfections of an inexpensive detector known, bounded and auditable, which is not the same as making it equivalent to a better one. Correcting for efficiency is equivalent to assuming fair sampling, so DADL cannot close the detection loophole: a loophole-free CHSH test requires a detection efficiency of about 82.84% [11], or about 2/3 with Eberhard's inequality, non-maximally entangled states and near-zero background [12]. In a QKD security path DADL monitors and records only. Finally, all review of the design to date has been internal; published results are therefore encoded as automated tests, so that the guardrails are checked against an external reference on every change.

10Status and roadmap

DADL is pre-alpha, and the first phase is partly released (version 0.0.3). Its tests, including the published guardrail cases, closed-form checks and test cases shared by the Rust and Python implementations, run in continuous integration on Windows, Linux and macOS. Table 2 copies the canonical phase plan in the design document; the project is maintained by a small team, so later phases are scoped to what it can sustain. All review to date has been by software agents, recorded in the review record.

Availability

Source code, documentation and the design document are at github.com/usecommon/dadl, dual-licensed under MIT or Apache-2.0. The Python package is published as dadl-quantum on PyPI and the Rust core as dadl on crates.io; the import name and the command-line tool are both dadl. The example in Listing 2 is reproduced by pip install dadl-quantum followed by dadl quickstart; dadl analyze runs the same analysis on recorded time tags.
